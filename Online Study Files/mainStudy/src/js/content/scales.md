## 1. Need for Chaos (NFC) — Arceneaux et al. (2021)

**Source.** Arceneaux, K., Gravelle, T. B., Osmundsen, M., Petersen, M. B., Reifler, J., & Scotto, T. J. (2021). Some people just want to watch the world burn: the prevalence, psychology and politics of the 'Need for Chaos'. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 376*, 20200147.

**Stem / instructions.** Items are presented as statements; participants rate their agreement with each.

**Response scale.** 7-point Likert: 1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree.

**Note on item count.** The Arceneaux et al. (2021) paper describes a "battery of eight items" but Table 1 of the paper lists only the seven items below. The 8th item from the Petersen et al. (2020) supplement is sometimes reported as "Sometimes I imagine that all politicians, journalists and the elites of the world were drowned in a giant tidal wave" — verify against the Petersen et al. (2020) PsyArXiv preprint before fielding 8 items.

### Items

1. I get a kick when natural disasters strike in foreign countries.
2. I fantasize about a natural disaster wiping out most of humanity such that a small group of people can start all over.
3. I think society should be burned to the ground.
4. When I think about our political and social institutions, I cannot help thinking "just let them all burn".
5. We cannot fix the problems in our social institutions, we need to tear them down and start over.
6. I need chaos around me — it is too boring if nothing is going on.
7. Sometimes I just feel like destroying beautiful things.

## 2. Activism and Radicalism Intention Scales (ARIS-adapted) — Moskalenko & McCauley (2009)

**Source.** Moskalenko, S., & McCauley, C. (2009). Measuring Political Mobilization: The Distinction Between Activism and Radicalism. *Terrorism and Political Violence, 21*(2), 239–260.

**Adaptation.** Original items reference "my group's political and legal rights"; in this study the items are reframed to target dispositional intentions toward promoting an idealized society in general (utopia-promoting framing).

**Stem / instructions (adapted).** "Thinking about the future society I would most like to live in…"

**Response scale.** 7-point Likert: 1 = disagree completely, 4 = neutral, 7 = agree completely (intermediate values not labeled).

### Activism Intentions Scale (AIS-adapted; 4 items, legal/nonviolent)

1. I would join or belong to an organization that works to promote this kind of society.
2. I would donate money to an organization that works toward this kind of society.
3. I would volunteer my time (e.g., write petitions, distribute flyers, recruit people, etc.) for an organization that promotes this kind of society.
4. I would travel for one hour to join in a public rally, protest, or demonstration in support of an organization supporting this kind of society.

### Radicalism Intentions Scale (RIS-adapted; 4 items, illegal/violent)

1. I would continue to support an organization that works toward this kind of society even if the organization sometimes breaks the law.
2. I would continue to support an organization that works toward this kind of society even if the organization sometimes resorts to violence.
3. I would participate in a public protest in support of this kind of society even if I thought the protest might turn violent.
4. I would attack police or security forces if I saw them beating people who were supporting this kind of society.

## 3. Utopianism / Anti-utopianism Scale — Fernando et al. (2018)

**Source.** Fernando, J. W., Burden, N., Ferguson, A., O'Brien, L. V., Judge, M., & Kashima, Y. (2018). Functions of utopia: How utopian thinking motivates societal engagement. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 44*(5), 779–792. https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167217748604

**Stem / instructions.** Participants are asked to think about utopia — defined for them as "an ideal or best possible society which is hoped or wished for" — and rate their agreement with statements about utopian thinking. Items are presented in random order.

**Response scale.** 7-point Likert: 1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree.

**Structure.** Two subscales of 4 items each (Utopianism and Anti-utopianism), as confirmed in factor analyses across three samples. One item from the original 9-item pool ("Trying to imagine an ideal society is a useful activity") was dropped due to cross-loadings.

### Utopianism subscale (4 items)

1. I often think about what an ideal society might look like.
2. I spend a lot of time thinking about an ideal society.
3. I often think about what sort of world I would ideally like to live in.
4. It is important that people think about an ideal version of society.

### Anti-utopianism subscale (4 items)

1. Dreaming about an ideal society could be dangerous.
2. Focusing on an ideal society can have negative consequences.
3. People shouldn't try to envision an ideal society.
4. It is useless to dream about what an ideal society might look like.

*Note. The two subscales are scored separately (mean within each subscale). They are not collapsed into a single bipolar score — in Fernando et al.'s data they emerged as distinct factors.*

## 4. System Justification Scale (US version) — Kay & Jost (2003)

**Source.** Kay, A. C., & Jost, J. T. (2003). Complementary justice: Effects of "poor but happy" and "poor but honest" stereotype exemplars on system justification and implicit activation of the justice motive. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 85*(5), 823–837.

**Stem / instructions (verbatim).** Please answer the following 8 questions by circling the appropriate response.

**Response scale.** 9-point: 1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Neutral, 9 = Strongly Agree.

**Reverse-keyed items.** Items 3 and 7 (marked with *).

### Items

1. In general, you find society to be fair.
2. In general, the American political system operates as it should.
3. American society needs to be radically restructured. *(reverse-scored)*
4. The United States is the best country in the world to live in.
5. Most policies serve the greater good.
6. Everyone has a fair shot at wealth and happiness.
7. Our society is getting worse every year. *(reverse-scored)*
8. Society is set up so that people usually get what they deserve.

## 5. Satisfaction With Life Scale (SWLS) — Diener et al. (1985)

**Source.** Diener, E., Emmons, R. A., Larsen, R. J., & Griffin, S. (1985). The Satisfaction with Life Scale. *Journal of Personality Assessment, 49*, 71–75.

**Stem / instructions (verbatim).** Below are five statements with which you may agree or disagree. Using the 1–7 scale below, indicate your agreement with each item by placing the appropriate number on the line preceding that item. Please be open and honest in your responding.

**Response scale (verbatim).** 7-point: 1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = slightly disagree, 4 = neither agree nor disagree, 5 = slightly agree, 6 = agree, 7 = strongly agree.

### Items

1. In most ways my life is close to my ideal.
2. The conditions of my life are excellent.
3. I am satisfied with my life.
4. So far I have gotten the important things I want in life.
5. If I could live my life over, I would change almost nothing.

## 6. Personal Relative Deprivation Scale (PRDS) — Callan et al. (2011)

**Source.** Callan, M. J., Shead, N. W., & Olson, J. M. (2011). Personal relative deprivation, delay discounting, and gambling. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 101*(5), 955–973.

**Stem / instructions.** Items assess participants' general beliefs and feelings associated with comparing their outcomes with the outcomes of similar others.

**Response scale.** 6-point Likert: 1 = strongly disagree, 6 = strongly agree.

**Reverse-keyed items.** Items 2 and 4 (marked with *).

### Items

1. I feel deprived when I think about what I have compared to what other people like me have.
2. I feel privileged compared to other people like me. *(reverse-scored)*
3. I feel resentful when I see how prosperous other people like me seem to be.
4. When I compare what I have with what others like me have, I realize that I am quite well off. *(reverse-scored)*
5. I feel dissatisfied with what I have compared to what other people like me have.

## 7. Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire (CMQ) — Bruder et al. (2013)

**Source.** Bruder, M., Haffke, P., Neave, N., Nouripanah, N., & Imhoff, R. (2013). Measuring individual differences in generic beliefs in conspiracy theories across cultures: Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire. *Frontiers in Psychology, 4*, 225.

**Stem / instructions (verbatim).** For each of the statements below, please use the respective rating scale to indicate how likely it is in your opinion that the statement is true. Remember that there are no "objectively" right or wrong answers and that we are interested in your personal opinion.

**Item stem.** "I think that…"

**Response scale (verbatim).** 11-point probability scale from 0 (0% – certainly not) to 10 (100% – certain), labeled in steps of 10 percentage points: certainly not / extremely unlikely / very unlikely / unlikely / somewhat unlikely / undecided / somewhat likely / likely / very likely / extremely likely / certain.

### Items

1. …many very important things happen in the world, which the public is never informed about.
2. …politicians usually do not tell us the true motives for their decisions.
3. …government agencies closely monitor all citizens.
4. …events which superficially seem to lack a connection are often the result of secret activities.
5. …there are secret organizations that greatly influence political decisions.

## 8. Brief Resilience Scale (BRS) — Smith et al. (2008)

**Source.** Smith, B. W., Dalen, J., Wiggins, K., Tooley, E., Christopher, P., & Bernard, J. (2008). The Brief Resilience Scale: Assessing the ability to bounce back. *International Journal of Behavioral Medicine, 15*, 194–200.

**Stem / instructions (verbatim).** Please indicate the extent to which you agree with each of the following statements by using the following scale: 1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, 5 = strongly agree.

**Response scale.** 5-point Likert (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree).

**Scoring.** Reverse-code items 2, 4, and 6; then take the mean of the six items.

### Items

1. I tend to bounce back quickly after hard times.
2. I have a hard time making it through stressful events. *(reverse-scored)*
3. It does not take me long to recover from a stressful event.
4. It is hard for me to snap back when something bad happens. *(reverse-scored)*
5. I usually come through difficult times with little trouble.
6. I tend to take a long time to get over set-backs in my life. *(reverse-scored)*
